How I Created My Own Frankenstein's Monster to Become an AI Supervillain

Listen
August 15, 2026

Every respectable supervillain needs a secret lair. Mine started as a tiny Blackstorm gaming PC I bought years ago from Verkkokauppa.

I replaced its tired RTX 3060 Ti with a massive RTX 3090. The original power supply choked on the new GPU. After hours of hacking, I was forced to wire an 850W ATX power supply entirely outside the casing. It looked like a madman’s experiment — blinking mysteriously in a chaotic rainbow of RGB lights, cables spilling across my desk like veins.

I booted it up and typed: “Are you a human-like entity?”

The model inside the GPU, Qwen 3.6 27B, went full pace into denial:

“No, no noooooo and once again NO! I am just a machine, not human-like at all…”

I sat there staring at the screen, then at that exposed, pulsing hardware on my desk, feeling a strange sense of cognitive dissonance. A hacked-together pile of blinking metal was arguing philosophy with me, entirely offline, completely private.

That Frankenstein PC was the prototype. It was the moment I realized: if I scaled this, I could build something no SaaS subscription could touch.

Here’s how that blinking pile of parts evolved into a 72GB distributed VRAM agentic swarm — and why it’s an absolute business advantage.

The Lair

When people talk about AI infrastructure today, they mean Kubernetes clusters, Azure regions, or AWS Availability Zones.

I mean four computers in my house.

My Agent Orchestrator doesn’t live on a laptop. It doesn’t live in the cloud. It lives across four machines, each doing exactly what it’s good at — coordinated like a single brain.

Machine CPU GPU RAM Role
Laptop Intel (laptop) Integrated 16GB OpenCode UI, Git, Agent Orchestrator
Desktop Ryzen 5 5600X RTX 3090 24GB 16GB Gitea, systemd services, Proton Bridge, STT/TTS
Bonobo Ryzen 9 3900 2x RTX 3090 24GB (1 internal + 1 external chassis) 64GB Qwen 3.6 27B via llama.cpp (LLM inference)
Dell 2x Xeon Silver 4210R None (Matrox G2000eW3) 62GB Autonomous OpenCode server, vector search, persistent sessions

Four machines. One network. Zero cloud dependency for the core loop.

The Bonobo Problem

Bonobo is the muscle — the inference beast. But here’s where the story gets interesting.

I wanted maximum VRAM for running large models. Two RTX 3090s give me 48GB of VRAM — blistering fast for a 27B model, with headroom to run 70B+ quantized models or multiple agents concurrently.

But the second 3090 simply didn’t fit inside Bonobo’s case.

The internal space was maxed out with one card already occupying two slots. So I did what any reasonable supervillain would do: I wired up an external GPU chassis, connected it via long PCIe cables, and plugged in the second card. One 3090 lives inside the case. The other sits beside it like a mechanical heart transplant, pulsing with its own power supply and RGB lights — very much like that original Frankenstein PC on my desk.

Dr. Frankenstein sourced body parts from graveyards. I sourced GPUs from sales. Same energy.

Why Four Machines?

A single powerful machine sounds simpler. But splitting workloads gives me isolation, reliability, and cost efficiency.

Laptop: The brain’s cortex. This is where I interact with the system via OpenCode. It runs the orchestrator logic, manages sessions, and pushes code to Gitea. Lightweight, mobile, always on.

Desktop: The nervous system. All background services live here — systemd units for email sync, browser automation, WhatsApp bridges, speech synthesis. An RTX 3090 sits idle enough that it could handle embedding models or a dedicated vision assistant. If the desktop reboots, the laptop keeps working. If the laptop sleeps, the desktop keeps running cron jobs.

Bonobo: The muscle. Two RTX 3090s with 48GB of combined VRAM can run Qwen 3.6 27B locally at blazing speed. When I’m not chatting, it idles cheaply. No per-token API bill. No rate limits.

Dell: The independent mind. With dual Xeon Silver 4210R CPUs (40 threads) and 62GB of RAM, Dell is its own autonomous OpenCode server — complete with local Qdrant for vector search, systemd-managed services, and persistent sessions that survive laptop shutdowns. While I sleep, Dell keeps thinking.

The Code Flow: One-Way Street

Security matters even in a homelab. I enforce a strict direction for code changes:

Laptop → Gitea (on Desktop) → Desktop pulls + restarts services

Python code never commits from the desktop. The laptop is the only write path. This prevents accidental corruption and keeps deployment predictable.

Network Architecture

Everything talks over SSH. No exposed ports, no firewalls to configure. Just keys and trust.

Each remote host has a dedicated Python module with SSH tools — ssh_exec, ssh_status, ssh_upload — wrapped in decorators so the LLM can call them like any other tool. The orchestrator doesn’t care if a function runs locally or on Bonobo 2 meters away. It just calls it.

This abstraction is key: the LLM sees tools, not hosts. It doesn’t know that deploy_to_remote involves Git pushes and systemd restarts across two machines. It just knows it can do the thing.

Why Not Just Use a Cloud VM?

I’ve tried. Azure, AWS, Render — all fine for demos. But here’s what happens at scale:

  1. Data leaves your network: Every tool call, every email search, every WhatsApp message crosses public internet
  2. Latency compounds: 1800+ tools × round-trip latency = noticeable delay per turn
  3. Cost accumulates: A $5 VPS sounds cheap until you add egress fees, managed databases, and API tokens

Local hardware has one cost: electricity. After that, it’s free forever.

What Would Break If One Machine Dies?

Good question. The system is designed to degrade gracefully:

  • Laptop dies: Desktop keeps running cron jobs, email sync, browser automation. No inference happens, but nothing breaks.
  • Desktop dies: Laptop can still chat with the LLM on Bonobo. Background services pause. WhatsApp/email go silent.
  • Bonobo dies: Orchestrator works locally with cached tools. Inference fails, so no new AI responses. You can switch to a cloud model as fallback.
  • Dell dies: Vector search pauses, but inference and background services keep running.

No single point of failure kills everything. That’s the point of distribution.

The Ultimate NDA: What Happens in the Lair, Stays in the Lair

Every respectable mastermind knows one fundamental truth: you don’t outsource your master plan to a mega-corporation. Right now, the tech industry is in a state of quiet panic. CTOs and founders want the unprecedented speed of AI coding assistants, but they are terrified of pasting their proprietary, million-dollar codebases into Claude or ChatGPT.

They worry about their code being used as training data for future models. They fear corporate espionage or data breaches at third-party vendors. Many are bound by strict NDAs, GDPR, or HIPAA regulations that legally forbid them from sending data outside their own servers.

That is the true superpower of this Frankenstein infrastructure. It isn’t just about raw speed — it’s an impenetrable fortress.

When my agentic swarm goes to work on a client’s project, it operates completely off the grid. The Dell server maps the entire architecture in its vector database, and Bonobo’s dual RTX 3090s churn through complex logic, all without a single byte of telemetry leaving my local network. There are no API calls beamed to servers in California. No cloud vendor scraping your IP.

When you hire me, you get the output of a state-of-the-art AI development team with an absolute, ironclad zero-data-leakage guarantee. I don’t just sign your NDAs — I enforce them at the hardware level.

From Monster to Mastermind

That Frankenstein PC with its external power supply taught me something important: raw compute is nothing without orchestration. A monster is just brute force until you give it direction.

What I built isn’t a messy pile of hardware. It’s a 100% private, zero-latency autonomous coding agency. When my orchestrator spins up Qwen to write, test, and debug code overnight, there are no rate limits throttling my agents and no cloud telemetry scraping my clients’ intellectual property.

Every enterprise faces three problems right now: exorbitant API costs, vendor lock-in, and the terrifying reality of data leaks. My “villain lair” solves all three.

It turns out, building a supervillain-tier AI home lab isn’t just an eccentric hobby. It’s an uncompromising business advantage.
